Act 01 the vision — the first thing I'd do

Drexel is an
archipelago. I'd build the bridge.

Two years here taught me one thing: Design, Business, Engineering, and the Sciences all orbit the same campus without ever touching. My first move as a Campus Leader is a cross-college Creative Jam — one room, every major, everyone making something in Creative Cloud. Engineers geeking out over Generative Fill. Business majors discovering Firefly. Designers stealing a stats trick from a scientist. Design is the common language; I just want everyone speaking it.

fig. 01 — the campus, connected move your cursor · the network responds

Why it works — everyone needs design at some point, whether it's an AI-generated mockup or an idea sketched on a napkin. A Creative Jam gives every college a reason to be in the same room for the first time.

Then what — monthly cross-major crit nights, a Firefly workshop series that travels college to college, and a semester-end showcase where an engineer's project sits beside a designer's. And I'm not starting from zero: my fraternity is a ready-made crew for turnout and promotion. The bridge stays built.

Act 02 my creative world

My studio has
a skyline.

I picked a school in this city on purpose. Philadelphia is a rotating exhibit of creative people doing their thing, and I just try to be in the room for it. A photography class here rewired how I see — it was basically an excuse to wander, and now I can't walk anywhere without noticing light, color, or some detail I need to shoot. My TikTok, Instagram, and Pinterest folders are a curated well of inspiration, and I'm the one dragging friends from other majors along to all of it.

Act 03 my take on AI

The tool ends.
The artist begins.

What excites me: projects that took hours now come together in a single prompt, freeing time for actual creativity instead of grunt work. What makes me hesitant: the abuse of it — AI slop churned out because it's cheap, not because it serves the work. That's not innovation; that's a shortcut with no soul.

When I talk about AI with students, I frame it as a before-and-after: what did this workflow look like without the tool, and what does it look like now? The same particles, arranged with intent or without it. Press the buttons — you'll see the difference.

“Where's the line between AI helping the work and AI replacing the thinking?” the conversation I want to lead on campus — not “AI good” or “AI bad.”
